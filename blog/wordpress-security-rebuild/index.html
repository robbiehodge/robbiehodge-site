Malicious Code Is the Best Redesign Budget
You Never Asked For.

Author
Robbie Hodge
Published
October 2026
Read time
5 minutes
Topic
Web Strategy · Security

I have been talking to a client about their website for a while. The old site worked, technically. It loaded. The pages were there. But it was built on WordPress, running plugins that had not been updated in years, sitting on hosting that nobody had looked at closely in longer than anyone could remember.

The redesign conversation had come up. It kept getting deprioritized. There was always something more urgent, more clearly tied to revenue, easier to justify. The website was not broken enough to fix.

Then malicious code showed up in the codebase.

By Tuesday of this week, the new site was live. Clean codebase, better performance, nothing legacy left behind. The project that had been stalled for months shipped in days.

“Nobody wants to pay for a redesign until something breaks. Then it is urgent. Security problems are the fastest way to get a stalled project approved.”

The Pattern That Keeps Repeating

This is not the first time I have watched this happen and it will not be the last. The pattern is consistent across clients of every size and industry: the website gets deprioritized until something goes wrong, and then it becomes the most urgent thing in the room.

It is not a bad thing, exactly. Urgency is useful. When a site gets compromised, decisions get made fast. Approvals that would have taken three months of internal debate happen in an afternoon. Budgets get unlocked. Everyone agrees the old site needs to go.

The bad part is that the urgency is reactive. You are now rebuilding under pressure, with a compromised site potentially affecting real visitors, with your team scrambling instead of executing a plan.

The rebuild happens either way. The only question is whether you do it on your terms or theirs.

What Actually Goes Wrong With Old WordPress Sites

Old WordPress installations are not inherently insecure. The problem is the combination of factors that tend to accumulate over time on sites nobody is actively maintaining:

The typical failure stack

Outdated core WordPress version · Abandoned or unpatched plugins · Themes with known vulnerabilities · Shared hosting with weak isolation · No monitoring or alerting · Credentials that have not changed in years · No backup strategy that has ever been tested

Any one of these alone is manageable. All of them together, on a site nobody is watching, is a question of when not if. Malicious actors scan for exactly this combination at scale. They are not targeting your site specifically. They are running automated scans that find the gaps and exploit them.

When it hits, the damage varies. Sometimes it is invisible code that turns your site into a spam relay. Sometimes it is a redirect that sends your visitors somewhere else entirely. Sometimes you only find out because Google flags your site in Search Console or a visitor tells you something looked wrong.

The Right Way to Rebuild After a Compromise

When a site gets hit, the instinct is to clean it. Scan for malware, remove the injected code, patch the vulnerability, move on. That works sometimes. More often, once a site has been compromised, you do not fully know the extent of what was touched or left behind. Cleaning a compromised site is harder than it sounds, and the confidence level afterward is never as high as it should be.

The cleaner path is a full rebuild on a clean codebase. You know exactly what is in it because you built it. There is no legacy code you are not sure about, no plugin you forgot was there, no configuration inherited from a developer who left two years ago.

That is what we did. New codebase, clean hosting, nothing carried over from the old build. The site launched this week and it is fast, clean, and we know exactly what is in it.

“Cleaning a compromised site gives you less confidence than you want. A clean rebuild gives you exactly what you built. Nothing more, nothing less.”

The Lesson About How Decisions Actually Get Made

There is a broader point here about organizational decision-making that I keep coming back to.

Most businesses are not short on ideas for things that should be improved. They are short on urgency. The website redesign, the CRM migration, the marketing overhaul that has been on the roadmap for two years -- these things move when something forces the issue. A competitor launches something that makes you look outdated. A tool breaks publicly in front of a prospect. Malicious code shows up in your codebase.

If you are a consultant or an internal leader trying to get something approved, one of the most useful things you can do is make the risk visible before the incident makes it undeniable. Show the exposure. Quantify what a compromise or a failure would cost. Make the case that the question is not whether to act but when, and that acting now costs less than acting later under duress.

The security incident did not create the problem. It just made the problem impossible to ignore any longer. The vulnerability was always there. The risk was always real. The stalled project was always overdue.

The site is live now. It is better than what it replaced. And we did not have to wait for something to go wrong to make the case for building it right.

If your site has been sitting on an old stack nobody has touched in years, that is worth looking at before something finds it first.
