The things that stood out

Four places I'd start.

These are observations from the public site and ads. The accounts will tell us which changes are most valuable.

01
Paid pages + local positioning

Your Matthews concern shows up in the paid pages, too.

The drain, general plumbing and water-heater landing pages I reviewed all lead with Matthews. Charlotte and the South Carolina service areas appear farther down.

If someone in Charlotte or SC clicks an ad and lands on one of these default pages, I'd test a version that immediately speaks to their service and location. The account will show where those clicks are actually going and whether other versions are in use.

First move: Match the search, ad and landing page to the job and territory you want.

02
Mobile experience

The paid drain page needs some mobile attention.

The homepage tested well. The separate drain advertising page scored lower and had substantial movement while the page loaded, with the diagnostic pointing toward the form area.

First move: Stabilize the form, retest on phones and measure whether more of the right visitors become booked jobs.

03
The question for the accounts

Follow the extra spend all the way to a completed job.

When more budget stops producing the growth you expect, I'd look at what changed in the additional traffic: services, search terms, geography, new versus returning customers, and how those inquiries were handled.

Ad click→Qualified inquiry→Booked visit→Completed job

Your public site shows signs of call tracking and links to ServiceTitan. I'd verify that those pieces connect properly so we can separate a phone tap, an actual inquiry and a completed sale.

First move: Compare results before and after the budget increases by service and market, including cost per completed new-customer job.

04
From interest to an appointment

Make the next step clear and easy.

On the main drain page, the header booking button opens a contact form while the main booking button goes to ServiceTitan. The paid water-heater form requires six fields. There are useful opportunities to test clearer labels, fewer required fields and a clear follow-up expectation.

I'd also check call answering, missed-call follow-up and what happens outside office hours. Those need account and office data; they aren't things the website alone can prove.

First move: Trace 20 to 30 recent inquiries with the office to find where the handoff or tracking needs attention.

What I could see in your public advertising

Google's advertiser library listed 10 ads under the U.S. / Any time filters. Seven sampled entries showed a last-shown date of October 12. One preview pointed to the separate offers.mdplumbingco.com site.

That gives us a view of the messaging. It doesn't disclose your actual search terms, budgets, bidding settings or results, and 10 library entries does not mean 10 active campaigns.

A few ideas beyond buying more of the same clicks
  • Follow up on unsold estimates. See whether a clear explanation, a timing question or a useful follow-up can recover opportunities you've already paid to generate.
  • Separate job economics. Treat a drain visit, a water-heater replacement and a major sewer repair according to their actual margins and completed-job rates.
  • Show the people behind the company. Your community involvement gives us real material for owner and technician videos, local job stories and useful repair explanations.
  • Use call review to sharpen the strategy. With appropriate access, use AI to help group service needs and reasons customers didn't book, then have a person validate the findings.
